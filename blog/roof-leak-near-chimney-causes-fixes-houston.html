Roof Leak Near Chimney:
Causes and Fast Fixes

Noticed damp drywall around your fireplace breast or water marks running down attic chimney brickwork? Chimneys penetrate your roof deck, creating one of the most leak-prone intersections on any Houston home. Here is how to diagnose chimney flashing failures and fix them quickly.

📅 Published: August 1, 2026 ⏱️ 9 Min Read 🏷️ Category: Maintenance Guides

When heavy rain pours over Houston, your roof’s primary job is to shed water downward off asphalt shingles into gutters. But wherever a vertical masonry structure—like a brick fireplace chimney—punches straight through that sloped roof deck, the smooth flow of water is disrupted.

Water rushing down the roof slope hits the back wall of the chimney, creating an intense pool of runoff. Without a watertight seal between rigid masonry bricks and flexible roofing shingles, water quickly seeps into attic joists, dampening ceiling sheetrock and staining living room walls around the fireplace.

If you are dealing with a roof leak near your chimney, applying a quick smear of caulk from a tube is rarely a permanent solution. This comprehensive guide examines the four main structural failure points around Houston chimneys and outlines the exact professional repair steps needed to keep your home completely dry.

Why Chimney Roof Intersections Leak So Frequently

Your house expands and contracts constantly. During hot 95°F summer afternoons in Houston, brick chimneys expand at a different rate than the wood rafters and plywood roof decking supporting your shingles. This natural thermal expansion and contraction puts enormous mechanical stress on the metal seals connecting the roof to the chimney.

Over time, caulking degrades, metal strips pull away from mortar joints, and heavy downpours force water through microscopic gaps. When leaks occur, securing specialized roof repair solutions addresses both the metal flashing assemblies and the underlying roof decking before wood rot sets in.

The 4 Primary Failure Points Causing Chimney Roof Leaks

A proper chimney seal consists of multiple overlapping metal components working together. When a leak develops near your chimney, it usually stems from failure in one of these four specific components:

Flashing Component Location & Function Common Failure Mode Repair Priority
Step Flashing L-shaped metal woven between shingle courses along chimney sides Rusting galvanized metal, missing fasteners, or shingle detachment HIGH (Direct water path into attic)
Counter (Cap) Flashing Metal bent into brick mortar joints overhanging step flashing Mortar crumbling, caulking decay, or metal pulling out of cut reglets HIGH (Allows rain behind step metal)
Chimney Cricket (Saddle) Small peak structure behind chimneys wider than 30 inches Missing cricket causing standing water pooling and valley seam rot CRITICAL (Dam water accumulation)
Apron & Base Flashing Single metal sheet spanning front chimney wall across shingles Thermal warping, lifted nail heads, or degraded polyurethane sealant MODERATE (Front wall discoloration)

1. Compromised Counter-Flashing and Mortar Reglets

Counter-flashing is the metal cap embedded directly into the chimney’s horizontal mortar joints (called reglets). In older Houston homes, contractors often used cheap silicone or roofing tar to seal counter-flashing into place. Texas heat bakes silicone into a brittle strip within 3 to 5 years. Once the sealant splits, wind-driven rain running down the brickwork slips directly behind the flashing and into your attic.

2. Corroded or Improperly Woven Step Flashing

Along the sides of the chimney, individual 90-degree metal step flashing pieces must be woven underneath every single course of shingles. If an installer used a single long piece of metal instead of individual step pieces, roof movement will buckle the metal. Furthermore, galvanized steel step flashing prone to rust allows water penetration along side walls.

3. Lack of a Back-Side Chimney Cricket (Saddle)

According to Texas building codes, any chimney wider than 30 inches standing perpendicular to the roof slope requires a small pitched roof structure behind it called a chimney cricket or saddle. Without a cricket, the back of your chimney acts like a dam during heavy Houston thunderstorms. Water accumulates against the back brick wall, rising above the flashing height and pouring into the house.

4. Deteriorated Chimney Mortar and Concrete Crown

Sometimes the roof metal itself is perfectly intact, but the leak originates at the top of the chimney. Hairline cracks in the concrete chimney crown or deteriorating mortar joints allow rainwater to be absorbed directly into porous bricks like a sponge. Water travels downward through the inside of the brick structure and emerges near the roofline ceiling.

Emergency Steps: What to Do When a Chimney Leak Starts During a Storm

If rain is actively streaming down your chimney walls right now, taking immediate containment measures prevents catastrophic drywall loss:

  1. Catch & Contain Interior Runoff: Place heavy plastic sheeting over your fireplace mantle and floor. Set buckets under active ceiling drips.
  2. Relieve Trapped Ceiling Water: If ceiling sheetrock near the chimney is bulging or discolored, poke a small hole with a nail in the center of the wet mark to let water drain cleanly into a bucket.
  3. Request Emergency Tarping: If severe wind or fallen tree branches damaged chimney flashing during a thunderstorm, arranging temporary emergency roof tarping secures the chimney stack until weather permits full reconstruction.
  4. Document Damage for Insurance: Take photos of wet brickwork, ceiling stains, and damaged roof flashing for your claim file. If high winds tore off adjacent shingles, filing a Texas roof insurance claim can assist with restoration costs.

How Roofing Professionals Permanently Fix Chimney Roof Leaks

A lasting repair requires replacing failed components with heavy-gauge, corrosion-resistant metals and proper masonry techniques:

  • Grinding New Reglets: Technicians use a diamond blade angle grinder to cut a clean 1-inch deep groove into the brick mortar lines.
  • Installing Custom Lead or Aluminum Flashing: Custom-bent aluminum or lead counter-flashing is inserted deep into the cut groove and anchored with stainless steel masonry fasteners.
  • Tucking High-Grade Polyurethane Sealant: High-performance polyurethane masonry sealant is injected into the reglet joint, creating a 20-year watertight seal that withstands building expansion.
  • Framing a Custom Chimney Cricket: For chimneys lacking proper rear drainage, carpenters frame a custom plywood cricket covered with ice-and-water shield membrane and new shingles to divert runoff smoothly around both sides.

If high winds or hail damaged surrounding shingles during the same storm, performing full high-wind storm damage repairs ensures the entire roof slope remains completely watertight.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fix a chimney roof leak myself with roofing tar?

Smearing black roofing cement or caulk around a chimney is only a temporary patch that lasts a few months. Roofing tar traps moisture underneath the metal, accelerating rust and wood rot. Permanent repairs require replacing old step flashing and cutting fresh reglets into mortar.

How much does it cost to repair chimney roof flashing in Houston?

Minor counter-flashing resealing or single-side step flashing repairs typically range from $350 to $750. Full four-sided chimney flashing replacement, including grinding new reglets and replacing damaged surrounding shingles, generally ranges between $800 and $1,800 depending on roof pitch and chimney size.

How do I know if the leak is from roof flashing or the chimney crown?

If water appears immediately during heavy downpours, damaged roof flashing or a missing cricket is usually the culprit. If water dampens the chimney brickwork hours after rain stops or during prolonged light drizzles, water is likely soaking through a cracked concrete crown or porous brick mortar joints.

Does homeowners insurance pay for chimney leak repairs?

Insurance policies cover chimney roof repairs if the damage was caused by a sudden covered event, such as a fallen tree limb, hail impact, or wind tearing away flashing. Leaks caused by gradual mortar decay or old sealant failure are considered routine maintenance.
